## Why this step exists

Firmographics (industry, size, location) say who *could* buy. They do not say why the best customers bought. An ICP built on them alone returns lookalikes: companies that match on paper but have no particular reason to choose the client. This step finds the reason, in three parts, each answering a question the ICP must be able to answer.

| Part | Question | Feeds |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Alternatives and what only the client gives | What do buyers compare the client with, and what do they get only from the client? | Tier 1 criteria and the "Why it matters" column of the ICP table |
| 2. The pain you can see from outside | Which companies show that need in public data, before anyone has spoken to them? | The ICP sentence, the visible-pain row, readiness rules, Pack 1 triggers |
| 3. Candidate offers | What is a Tier 1 company that has never heard of the client offered first? | Pack 2, the client-facing summary, the validation plan |

Every row in this step follows the ground rules: a source or `[Assumption]`, a count (n) where the evidence is counted, and "none found" when nothing was found. Never fill a row from general knowledge of the market.

## Part 1: Alternatives and what only the client gives

1. **List the alternatives buyers actually considered.** Sources, strongest first: the "Alternatives" field of each transcript, question 2 of the five-minute kit, question 4 of the interview guide, lost-deal reasons, a lost-to field in the CRM, review sites. Always include doing nothing (staying with spreadsheets or a paper diary, asking an existing employee to cover it, putting up with the problem).
2. **Count each one (n) and give its sources.** Count an alternative only when a buyer named it or chose it. A competitor that appears on the client's own comparison page, or in your knowledge of the market, but in no buyer's evidence goes on a separate line: "named by the client, not by buyers".
3. **List what the client does that those alternatives do not.** Capabilities, terms, local presence, speed, the price model. Source each (case study, transcript, pricing page) or mark it `[Assumption]`.
4. **Turn each into the value the buyer gets.** "WhatsApp reminders" is a capability; "fewer empty appointment slots" is the value. Use the buyer's own words where the evidence has them.
5. **Ask who cares most about that value.** Which traits make a company value it more? For example, more branches mean more missed calls to lose; a dedicated practice manager means someone owns the problem. These traits are the Tier 1 criteria, and each gets its one-line reason in the ICP table's "Why it matters" column.

| Alternative buyers considered | n (sources) | What the client does that it doesn't | Value to the buyer | Evidence | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

If the evidence names no alternatives, say so in one line and go to "At Hypothesis tier" below.

## Part 2: The pain you can see from outside

A pain that shows in public data turns a list of lookalikes into a list of companies that are probably hurting now.

1. **Start from the pain-frequency table** (Step 2), with its n.
2. **For each candidate Tier 1 segment, find two to five public data heuristics:** things anyone can check from outside the company that imply the pain. Illustrative examples:

   | Pain | Heuristic | Source | Dated? |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Bookings lost to unanswered calls | Reviews saying nobody answers the phone | Google reviews | Yes: review date |
   | Front desk overstretched | Repeated job posts for receptionists | Job boards, careers page | Yes: posting date |
   | Growing beyond one site | A branch opened in the last year | Website, maps listing, press | Yes: opening date |
   | Compliance pressure | A licence renewal or new rule that applies to them | Regulator lists, official gazettes | Yes |
   | Manual process | Booking only by phone; no online form on the website | The company's own website | No |

3. **For each heuristic, record** its source, whether it carries a date, what links it to the pain (evidence, such as a transcript that mentions it or a won customer who showed it before buying, or your reasoning, marked `[Assumption]`), why it suggests the company may be ready to talk to a vendor *now*, and its reliability:

   | Reliability | Meaning |
   |---|---|
   | High | Dated, public and checkable, and seen before won deals in the look-back or named by a buyer as the reason they started looking |
   | Medium | Dated and checkable, but tied to the pain only by reasoning (`[Assumption]`) |
   | Low | Undated, hard to check, or easily explained by something other than the pain |

   **Where to look beyond LinkedIn and B2B databases.** The best signals are often in sources most researchers skip. Each must be checkable, and ideally dated:
   - regulator and licence registers, and their notices (new licences, upgrades, revocations, sanctions)
   - official gazettes, court and insolvency notices
   - public tenders and procurement portals
   - app stores: whether an app exists, its rating, and recent complaint reviews
   - job boards and careers pages: the roles, the tools named and how much hiring there is
   - review sites and maps listings: complaints and new locations
   - changes on the company's own site: a new branch, product or pricing page
   - press, funding and partnership announcements; conference speaker and exhibitor lists
   - partner and integration directories of the platforms the client works with
4. **Write the ICP sentence with this situation first**, then the firmographic boundary.
5. **No heuristic found?** Then the segment is a **pain hypothesis**. Say so in the ICP table, the ICP sentence and the sign-off. This label is separate from the evidence tier: a brief can be Grounded on customer evidence and still rest on a pain hypothesis.
6. **State how many won accounts the heuristics rest on**, against 20 to 30 where won-deal recordings exist. Heuristics from 4 won calls are a starting point, not a pattern.

Dated heuristics are the natural readiness rules in Step 7. Prefer them to a generic menu of signals (funding, hiring, a new leader) unless the evidence ties those signals to the pain. Where won and lost accounts with close dates exist, check the heuristics with the look-back in `scoring-and-validation.md` ("Readiness scoring"): did the event actually appear before the won deals more often than before the lost ones?

## Part 3: Candidate offers

The **offer in scope** (blocking question 1) is what the client sells. The **cold offer** is what a Tier 1 company that has never heard of the client is offered first: the reason to reply. ICP work usually skips it, and the messaging then decorates an offer nobody has tested.

1. **Split the client's value** (Part 1) into two to four candidate offers: one per distinct value, not a bundle.
2. **For each candidate offer give:**
   - **Type of value:** saves time, saves money, or makes money
   - **Frame:** sold as the solution itself, framed around the problem it solves, or something useful given away first (a free audit, benchmark or report, often called a lead magnet)
   - **Segment** it is for
   - **Proof** it rests on (case study, transcript with ID, a number the client can show), or `[Assumption]` if there is none
3. **Name the offer for Tier 1 companies that show no visible pain or trigger yet.** Usually most of a list shows no trigger at any given time, so this is the offer most accounts will see. It is the offer behind the scorer's "Contact now with the no-signal offer" action: those accounts are contacted now, not left to wait for a trigger.
4. **Offer status: untested** until each offer has been sent to real prospects and the replies counted. Show the status in the brief header, the ICP sign-off, Pack 2 and the client-facing summary. A resonance check (Step 10) is a sense check, not a test.

| Candidate offer | Value type | Frame | Segment | Proof it rests on | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

## At Hypothesis tier

Website-only work can still do this step, but most rows rest on the client's own claims. Say so rather than dressing them up.

- State the differentiated value as a **thesis**: "Thesis: [value], because [what the site shows, with URL]. To confirm: ask five customers what else they considered and why they picked the client."
- List alternatives found only on the client's site as "named by the client, not by buyers".
- Expect most visible-pain heuristics to be pain hypotheses at this tier, and label them.
- Give the website claim as the proof behind each candidate offer, and mark it `[Assumption]` when no customer evidence backs it.
- Put the confirming question in the sign-off and the validation plan.

---

## Client-type variants

- **B2B software:** alternatives are usually another tool, an in-house build, spreadsheets or doing nothing. Job posts that name the incumbent tool and review-site complaints are common visible-pain heuristics.
- **B2B services:** alternatives are an in-house hire, another agency, freelancers or doing nothing. Visible pain is often weaker, because services are bought on trust and referral. Expect more segments to stay pain hypotheses, and say so rather than quietly falling back to "one outcome in one vertical".
- **Local business / consumer:** alternatives are other local options, doing it at home, or going without. Visible pain is about places and groups, never about named people: competitors' reviews complaining about something the client does well, new housing or offices in the catchment, a season or event. The first offer is often a trial or a first visit.

## Where the results go

| Result | Where it appears |
|---|---|
| Alternatives, value, thesis | Brief section 4 "Why they buy"; ICP table rows "What they use today" and "Value they get that the alternative can't give" |
| Why each Tier 1 trait matters | ICP table, "Why it matters" column |
| Visible-pain heuristics, pain hypothesis | ICP sentence; ICP table row "Pain visible from outside"; Pack 1 trigger signals; readiness rules (Step 7) |
| Candidate offers and status | Brief header; Pack 2 "Offer inputs"; the client-facing summary in plain words; the validation plan (testing them with real sends) |
| All three, in short | The ICP sign-off (Step 6) |
